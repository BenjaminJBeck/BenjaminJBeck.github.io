Developmental Stress and Longevity: Testing the Role of Telomere Attrition in Lifespan Reduction
Ben Beck
Fall 2025
October 25, 2025

Abstract

It has long been disputed among scientists, healthcare professionals, and researchers whether it's 'nature' or 'nurture'. How tall you grow, how much you weigh, your mental health -- all depends on these two factors. It is also becoming increasingly apparent that lifespan also depends on these two. It is thought that developmental stress can influence cellular processes, immune functionality, and oxidative balances. However, the long-term consequences of these early development effects are not fully understood, there is a lot of research in this area currently ongoing. Across taxa, there are individuals that are exposed to poor conditions early in life, and often show signs of accelerated biological aging, including genetic signs such as shortened telomeres. Similarities in telomere length under similar conditions could suggest that physiological responses to early development stress may be a key determinant of aging and longevity. This research would investigate the mechanisms that link this stress to reduced lifespan specifically through telomere attrition. Using the model fruit fly Drosophila Melanogaster, developmental stress conditions will be experimentally manipulated to address two critical questions: 1) Does accelerated telomere attrition predict longevity? and 2) Does developmental stress lead to shortened telomeres? Also, keeping track of different kinds of stressors, we can also answer (though not critical to the study): 3) What major type of stressor is the most determinate of longevity in fruit flies? These fruit flies will be raised under varying conditions, including nutritional limitation, social crowding/absence, thermal stressors, air pressure changes, and predation. After a given age, random fruit flies from each stressor (as well as control group) will be analyzed for their telomere length. Hopefully, this data will provide critical insight into the evolution of aging and the critical factors of longevity from early life development.

Introduction

Aging is a widespread biological phenomenon across most multicellular organisms, though a few species such as Hydra exhibit negligible senescence, making the evolution of aging a long-standing puzzle in biology (Martínez, 1998). Evolutionary theories suggest, however, that aging is not just an inevitable consequence of living, but as a trait that is specifically selected for. There are three major evolutionary hypotheses that attempt to explain the evolution of senescence: mutation accumulation, antagonistic pleiotropy, and the disposable soma theory. The mutation accumulation theory suggests that negative mutations that affect late-life fitness accumulate, because selection also weakens with age (Medawar, 1952). Antagonistic pleitropy suggests that genes that are beneficial early in life may in fact be detrimental later in life, which shows trade offs between reproduction and longevity (Williams, 1957). And there is the disposable soma theory, which is specifically relevant to this research proposal. It suggests that organisms face fundamental trade-offs in how they are able to distribute limited resources through and between reproduction, growth, and cellular maintenance (Kirkwood & Holliday, 1979). Following the same logic, when organisms are faced with stressors, they have to prioritize energy in those situations over cellular maintenance -- for their survival but often leading to deterioration of cellular processes that help for longevity, such as telomeres. This ultimately contributes to the aging process.

Telomeres are a central part of understanding how aging works, and they are common to almost every vertebrate (Srinivas et al., 2020). They're the ends of the chromosomes that prevent shortening. During normal cell division, DNA polymerase cannot fully cover or replicate the ends, which leads to progressive shortening with each mitotic cycle. This problem, commonly known as the end replication problem, limits the amount of times cells can replicate before they reach the hayflick limit, until it reaches replicative senescence (Srinivas et al., 2020). Although there is an enzyme, called telomerase, that extends the telomeres, most somatic cells do not express this because it can ultimately lead to cancer, which means somatic cells are a result and consequence of aging. Telomeres are also pretty vulnerable to oxidative damage in part due to their high guanine content, and oxidative stress can accelerate telomere shortening, independent of the cell division (Nettle et al., 2017). When the telomeres become too short, they can trigger DNA damage responses that will ultimately lead to either senescence or apoptosis, which contributes to tissue dysfunction and aging (Rentscher et al., 2020).

The rate at which the telomeres shorten is not a constant speed, and can vary considerably individual to individual, influenced by environmental factors, especially stress. Chronic stress disrupts neuroendocrine balance and metabolism, and elevates oxidative stress and inflammation (Bobrovskikh & Gruntenko, 2023; Rentscher et al., 2020). These physiological changes are linked to faster telomere shortening and cellular aging (Shalev et al., 2013; Rentscher et al., 2020). There are multiple studies that show that when there is psychosocial stress, nutritional deprivation, or harsh environments, shorter telomeres are often associated. It is also important when these stressors occur. It has been shown that these stressors are more impactful and damaging in early development and can have lasting effects on telomere dynamics (Shalev et al., 2013). In this context with aging, early development stress could possibly establish cellular longevity trajectories that constrain adult lifespans, even if later-life conditions improve.

There is a growing body of evidence across taxa that developmental stress accelerates biological aging (Shalev et al., 2013; Rentscher et al., 2020; Nettle et al., 2017) In European starlings, nestlings that were raised under conditions of high sibling competition or nutritional stressors often showed significantly shorter telomeres, as well as elevated markers that oxidative stress had taken place, especially when compared to those raised under favorable conditions. These effects lasted longer than the period of stress during development, up through adulthood (Nettle et al., 2017). The study also found that both the amount of food that was provided as well as the begging effort required to obtain it independently accelerated telomere attrition, their effects being additive. In a similar fashion, in white throated sparrows, nestlings that were smaller than their siblings portrayed higher oxidative stress, and often associated with shorter telomeres (Grunst et al., 2019). These patterns can suggest that developmental stressors can create a lasting biological mark that influences the aging trajectories across a diverse taxa. In mammals, human studies have revealed that individuals who experienced childhood adversity, including neglect or some other types of stress in early life, have shorter telomeres in adulthood as well as higher risks of age-related diseases (Shavel et al., 2013). Remarkably, there are studies that show that even prenatal stressors can affect telomere length, showing that stress can affect telomere length and oxidative stress even before birth (Shavel et al., 2013). However, due to the complexity of human lives, given the many interacting variables in human lives, important questions remain unresolved. What remains unclear is whether developmental stress directly affects telomere shortening, or whether this telomere shortening directly causes reduced longevity.

In order for me to test the potential relationships between the developmental stress, telomere behavior, and the lifespan, a model organism -- such as the Drosophila Melangaster -- is necessary. Fruit flies are the perfect organism for an initial experiment because they have very short generation times and short lifespans, which allows complete life histories to be measured within reasonable timeframes. Their genetics and genomics are well known and measured, which makes it possible to measure different cellular markers such as telomere length with incredible precision. Fruit flies also show physiological responses to environmental stressors -- including changed metabolism, different oxidative stressors, and the activation of neuroendocrine signaling -- that parallel broad, conserved stress responses in other taxa (Bobrovskikh & Gruntenko 2023). Although the Drosophila can maintain telomeres using retrotransposons rather than telomerase, their telomeric structures are still functional to protect the chromosome ends and maintain its genome stability.

Previous research across multiple organisms have demonstrated that exposure to different stressors can often influence telomere behavior and interactions and aging outcomes. The early life stressors has been experimentally shown to accelerate cellular aging and increase inflammatory markers in different vertebrates (Nettle et al. 2017), and psychosocial or physiological stress is often associated with shorter telomeres and can reduce cellular functions (Shalev et al. 2013; Rentscher et al. 2020). Developmental stress has been linked to altered telomere dynamics in other species, which shows that stressful early life environments can affect how telomeres can change over time (Grunst et al. 2019). These studies all show that exposure to these stressors can shape both cellular aging pathways and later life physiological outcomes, which supports the rationale for experimentally manipulating developmental stress in Drosophila to test whether similar patters emerge.

Despite all these advances, few studies have examined whether developmental stress in Drosophila shortens telomeric sequences, and way fewer have evaluated whether telomere dynamics can moderate the relationship between stresses and adult lifespans. It is essential that this gap is realized and studied to determine whether different developmental origins of aging can operate through conserved cellular processes across taxa.

This research can address the fundamental questions in evolution and cell biology: Does developmental stressors shorten lifespan through accelerated telomere attrition? It is hypothesized here that fruit flies that are exposed to higher levels of developmental stress may exhibit significantly shorter telomeres and have reduced lifespans compared to controls raised under optimal conditions. Furthermore, it will be predicted that telomere length will actually mediate the relationship between stress exposure and overall longevity, showing that telomere attrition represents a mechanism pathway connecting environmental conditions to aging outcomes. By this experiment and manipulation of multiple categories of developmental stress-including nutrition limitations, social crowding, thermal fluctuation, and desiccation- this study will determine which forms of stress can most strongly impact telomere shortening and whether the combined stressors produce additive or synergistic effects. To understand how developmental environments shape the cellular aging trajectories can provide a critical insight into life-history evolution, aging mechanisms, and the basis of lifespan variation both within and amongst multiple species.

Specific Aims

Hypothesis 1: Exposure to developmental stress of early life accelerates telomere shortening in Drosophila melanogaster.

Specific Aim 1: Quantify telomere length in fruit flies raised under different categories of developmental stress, including social, nutritional, and environmental stressors.

Specific Aim 2: Determine if the rate of telomere shortening differs across the different categories of developmental stress.

Rationale: Developmental stress has been shown to increase oxidative stress and damages to DNA, which can contribute to telomere shortening and ultimately cellular aging (Nettle et al., 2017). By the experimental manipulation of levels and varying types of stress -- such as competition for food, crowding, thermal stress, air pressures, and predation- and comparing the telomere lengths across the different groups, we can test whether stress in early development directly accelerates telomere attrition. Fruit flies provide the ideal system for work because they can be raised under tightly controlled lab conditions with almost all components measurable and controllable.

Hypothesis 2: Accelerated telomere attrition caused by developmental stress can reduce lifespan in Drosophila melanogaster.

Specific Aim 1: Determine whether experimentally caused telomere shortening can predict early-life mortality risk independently of other physiological indicators of stress.

Specific Aim 2: Assess whether differences in telomere length among treatment groups predict adult lifespan and mortality rate.

Rationale: Shorter telomeres are often associated with premature cellular senescence and decreased lifespan across taxa (Rentscher et al., 2020; Shalev et al., 2013). If telomere shortening does in fact mediate the link between stress and longevity, individuals experiencing higher developmental stress should be shown both shorter telomeres and earlier mortality rates. By statistically correlating telomere length alongside longevity from each of the experimental and control groups, this study will show whether the telomere attrition serves as a mechanistic pathway connecting developmental stress to reduce longevity.

Proposed Methods

Study Systems

The organism for this study, Drosophila Melanogaster will be used to test whether early life stress shortens lifespan through the acceleration of telomere shortening. Fruit flies are the ideal model for this research due to their short lifespan, well characterized genome, and their sensitivity to environmental stressors -- alongside many other reasons. Using these flies can allow for large scale sample sizes and controlled lab conditions, which may minimize the external variability and also make statistical comparisons more robust.

The flies will be raised under standard lab conditions (25°C, 12:12 light-dark cycle) on standard cornmeal-agar diet, unless otherwise specified by experimental treatments. Approximately 500 individual fruit flies will be used in total, distributed evenly across treatment groups, as well as the control group, to ensure adequate replication and statistical analysis. The sample of 500 was selected because it produces powerful analysis considerations and uses of practical constraints due to its size, while keeping it within reasonable numbers, and will help us detect moderate effect sizes in telomere length and lifespan differences among groups, assuming standard variance exists in observed Drosophila aging studies. Each different treatment group will include equal numbers of males and females to account for sex conditions (15m, 15f). Sex is determined by general size, changes in the abdomen and legs (Cooper, n.d.). Within each of the treatment, individuals will be divided into cohorts: 30 for early adult telomere measurement, 30 for mid-life measurement, 20 for late life measurement, and the remaining individuals will be kept for complete lifespan tracking without telomere sampling to avoid disturbance related effects. This design balances the necessity for adequate replication with the reasonable constraints of large fly populations under controlled conditions.

Experimental Design

To test the effects of developmental stress, the fruit flies will be divided into eight different experimental treatments along with one control group, with each group representing a distinct type of early life stress. The control group will be maintained under SLC (standard laboratory conditions -- 25°C, 12:12 light and dark cycles, and standard cornmeal agar media) with no other imposed stressors, to establish a baseline value for the telomere length and lifespan. Nutritional stress will be induced by raising the larvae on media diluted to ½ of the normal nutrient concentration, which mimics early life stressors of food scarcity. Social crowding stressors will be created by decreasing environment tank size, increasing density of flies per area, creating a environment social crowding, which also forces individuals to compete for limited food and space. This causes elevated metabolic demand and creates oxidative stress. Thermal stress will be applied by exposing developing flies to fluctuating temperatures ranging from 18°C to 32°C each day, creating unpredictable environmental conditions that disrupt homeostasis. Desiccation stress will be induced through controlled exposure to more reduced humidity chambers, which produces mild dehydration, challenging water balance and cellular function. Air pressure stressors can be simulated by placing vials in sealed chambers subjected to short periods of increased pressure, a manipulation that can interfere with respiration. Predation stress will be simulated by introducing predator cues, such as exposure to lizards or snakes, which can introduce stress of prey defense and escape behaviors. Finally, a combined stress treatment will expose flies to all stressors simultaneously to assess the different additive or synergistic effects of multiple environmental challenges on telomere shortening and longevity.

These treatments represent ecologically relevant forms of stress known to influence oxidative balance and energy expenditure (Nettle et al., 2017; Grunst et al., 2019). Flies will remain in their assigned conditions from larval development until adulthood. Once emerges, adults will be maintained under identical, non-stressful conditions (Same conditions as the above "control" group) to isolate the effects of early-life stress.

Measurements

At predetermined time points (early adult stage, midlife, and old age), a random selection of individuals from each group (2 from each) will be collected for DNA extraction and telomere length measurements. Individual flies will be frozen and stored in liquid nitrogen at -80°C until the time of DNA extraction to preserve DNA integrity. DNA will be isolated from whole flies using a DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit following the manufacturer's protocol specifically for insects. DNA concentration and its purity will be concluded using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and samples will be standardized to 10 ng/μL. Telomere length will be measured using qPCR-based assays comparing the collected size to a model telomere size. The qPCR assay will measure relative telomere length by comparing the ratio of telomeric sequence copy number to a single-copy reference gene copy number, producing a ratio for each sample. Telomeric sequences will be amplified using primers designed to target Drosophila HeT-A retrotransposon sequences. The single-copy reference gene will be RpL32 (Ribosomal Protein L32), a commonly used Drosophila reference gene. All reactions will be performed in on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR system using SYBR Green master mix. Each 20 μL reaction will contain 10 μL SYBR Green master mix, 2 μL of DNA template (20 ng), 0.4 μL of each primer (10 μM), and 7.2 μL nuclease-free water. The cycling conditions will be 95°C for 10 minutes, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 seconds and 60°C for 1 minute. Standardized curves will be generated using dilutions of pooled DNA to ensure amplification efficiency is between 90-110% for both telomeric and reference gene assays. Samples will be randomized across plates, and each plate will include no-template controls and a reference sample to account for plate variation among the differing groups. Relative telomere length will be found by using the comparative Ct method, with shorter telomeres identified by higher Ct values and lower T/S ratios. All procedures will follow established telomere quantification procedures that are adapted from Shalev et al. (2013).

Lifespan Monitoring

The survival and lifespan will be recorded among all individuals of each different treatment group. Following the stopping of early life stressors, the now adult life flies will be sorted by sex under light CO₂ anesthesia, and housed in groups of 10 individuals per vial containing standard cornmeal-agar media. All the adults will be maintained under the same conditions regardless of their developmental treatments, to isolate the lasting effects of early life stress. Flies will be transferred to fresh media about every three days without anesthesia to minimize handling stressors. Mortality will also be monitored daily until the death of all individuals, which will allow for the creation of survival curves and estimation of mean and maximum lifespan per different treatment. Dead flies will be removed and recorded by sex, vial, and date. Any flies that are killed or have escaped will be removed from the study -- ignored for statistical purposes. Handling procedures will be standardized to ensure that everything is consistent across differing groups.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analyses will be performed using R studio. Mean telomere length and lifespan will be compared among treatments using ANOVA or other linear models, also depending on the data distribution. To test whether telomere attrition mediates the relationship that is found between stress and lifespan, regression analyses will be used with telomere length as a main predictor and lifespan as the main response variable.

Additional analyses will research the correlations between stressor's intensity and telomere shortening rates. Kaplan-Meier survival analyses and Cox proportional hazards models will assess the differences in mortality rates among the different treatments. A significance level of α = 0.05 will be used for all tests.

Conclusion / Significance

Understanding how the environmental stress during development can affect cellular aging is critical to explain the variation of lifespan among species. Although there are genetic factors that can influence longevity, there are also environmental and physiological conditions experienced early in life that can have lasting effects on the longevity components. Telomere attrition, a key biomarker of cellular aging, can provide a measurable link between the early life stress and the adult lifespan of the species (Shalev et al., 2013). Shortened telomeres also indicate cumulative oxidative damages and reduced repair capabilities, both of which can accelerate the tissue decline while increasing mortality risks (Shalev et al., 2013). Despite the increasing realization of this relationship, the casual connection between developmental stress, telomere loss, and lifespan remains partially not completely tested under controlled conditions.

This research will address this large gap by manipulating the stress levels in fruit flies, a main model organism ideal for studying the mechanisms of aging. By quantifying the telomere length and lifespan under different types of stress conditions, this study will directly try to find out whether developmental stress accelerates cellular aging. The use of multiple stress types -- social, nutritional, and environmental- allows the identification which challenges exert the most impact on telomere integrity and ultimately longevity.

From an evolutionary perspective, this research can contribute to understanding how different environmental pressures shape life history trade offs between the different types: growth, maintenance, and reproduction. If developmental stress directly leads to shorter telomeres and reduced lifespan, it may suggest that energy allocation during stressful early life periods prioritize short term survival energies over long term cellular maintenance, such as telomere maintenance and other types. If these are the results, such findings will support the disposable soma theory.

Beyond the evolutionary implications, the research will also hold relevance for biomedical science, as it parallels the developmental origins of health and disease frameworks in possibly humans. Insights from the fruit fly models can inform a better understanding of how stress can expose during critical periods of developmental influences of aging and disease susceptibility later in life. Overall, this project will advance the knowledge of the mechanisms linking developmental stress to aging, providing a foundation for future studies on the evolution of longevity.
